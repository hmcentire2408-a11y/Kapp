# AO Account Digest

Condensed summaries of seven practitioner posts from two former admissions
officers. Reddit interface text, ads, and moderator boilerplate stripped.
Everything here is paraphrased; nothing is quoted at length.

**Scope note.** Essay craft and evaluation logic have been extracted into
`essay-evaluator-lens.md` and are deliberately *not* repeated here. This
document keeps what the lens doesn't cover: process mechanics, extracurriculars,
scores, recommendations, school context, institutional priorities, and
everything else.

---

## Provenance and how much to trust this

Both authors are pseudonymous former admissions officers who now sell
consulting, which gives them an interest in the process appearing decipherable.
Where they corroborate the primary documents in this project — Harvard's Class
of 2023 Reading Procedures (`1333554.pdf`, an SFFA trial exhibit) and the CSCW
2018 cognitive task analysis of holistic review (`10284843.pdf`) — treat them as
reliable. Where they assert numbers, cutoffs, or population claims, treat them
as one office's impression.

**Specific cautions.** williamthereader's material on race-linked score bands is
pre-SFFA and pre-test-optional and is stated far more confidently than one
office's experience supports; don't rely on it. The two authors flatly
contradict each other on how much extracurriculars matter, which is probably a
real institutional difference rather than a general law.

**Known tensions with the primary documents.** Aggravating_Humor says top
offices assign no rating to essays; Harvard's procedures show no *essay* score
but make the essay the principal input to the Personal rating — the second
framing is more precise. The CSCW paper describes readers assessing whether a
university's mission resonates with the applicant; Aggravating_Humor explicitly
rejects that definition of fit in favor of internal consistency across the file.
Prefer his.

# u/williamthereader

## 1. AMA — Top-5 university alum, worked in alma mater's admissions office (Sept 2018)

**Who.** Asian American, average New Jersey public high school with few
admissions resources, no hooks, admitted to multiple Ivy-caliber schools. Worked
in his own school's admissions office as an undergraduate. At time of writing:
full-time finance, part-time consulting. Verified by moderators.

**Feeder and boarding schools.** His most forceful claim: roughly 10–15% of the
class each year comes from a set of around thirty private boarding and target
schools, mostly in New England, and those applicants face a materially lower bar
while skewing wealthy and white. He says the single most effective admissions
lever available to a family is enrolling at one of these schools. A Harvard
graduate pushed back in the comments, arguing those schools simply enroll better
students. He responded sharply: he read for Exeter and Andover, and the pattern
he saw was admits in the 1300–1400 range from those schools in years when
high-scoring public school students with state and national recognition were
rejected. His explanation for the placement gap is institutional relationship,
not student quality, and he pointed to high-scoring California public schools
losing out to boarding schools annually as the counterexample.

**Scores.** All thresholds he gives are hedged as loose and reader-dependent. A
former colleague of his used roughly 1520–1530 as a working floor for unhooked
white and Asian applicants; the ACT equivalent he cites is 34–35, split
dependent. He says the difference between a 1540 and a 1580 would not register
in committee. Superscoring is not penalized. He recommends the ACT as easier to
score well on and equally regarded. Timing matters in a way applicants don't
expect: if the office needs to raise its average late in the cycle it will, and
if it doesn't need to, it won't. He notes the office rejects roughly three
quarters of applicants with perfect scores.

**Why AP scores barely count.** This explanation is the most interesting thing
in the AMA and doesn't appear in the formal documents. Admissions hiring is
largely internal, and the people who take full-time admissions jobs are not
generally the strongest students from their graduating classes — the top
graduates go into finance, engineering, medicine. So the reader evaluating you
may have been admitted years ago with scores well below the current median, and
has little instinct to care whether you scored a 2400 or a 2370. He adds a
second mechanism: when every applicant looks identical on scores, readers stop
attending to scores and go looking for differentiation elsewhere. He mentions
having scored 800s on three subject tests and fives on eleven AP exams himself,
and how that felt to learn afterward.

**Extracurriculars.** He believes the subreddit dramatically overrates them.
Asked to name what actually drives an application, he answered: story, plus
race, high school, and connections — everything else matters only somewhat.
Generic officer positions are commonplace: he points out there are tens of
thousands of high schools and therefore tens of thousands of NHS presidents
competing for two thousand seats. Internships mostly don't register, and he
notes an anti-corporate streak in his office; the exception is an internship
that tells a story, and his example is a student passionate about photography
who interned with a well-known nature photographer and travelled — that mattered
because it cohered. Interning at a parent's firm would not.

**Awards.** School-level honors and even National Merit are, in his account,
near-invisible from repetition. Genuine national competition results — he names
Tournament of Champions debate, DECA nationals, USACO, Intel — do register. But
he attaches a warning: applicants with those credentials frequently fail anyway
because they read as high-achieving machines rather than people, and making
yourself legible as a person becomes the harder task.

**Volunteering.** Anything over roughly 250 hours reads as solid, but what you
did matters considerably more than the total.

**Recommendations.** What separates a strong letter from a generic one is not
closeness to the teacher but content. Most letters cover academic performance
and classroom behavior and blur together. A letter containing a specific story,
or describing a struggle, is the kind of thing that stays with a reader and can
reframe how the entire file is read. Average letters, he says, are not a serious
problem.

**Committee mechanics.** Three gating checks: can the student handle the
coursework, do they clear score minimums, and do they actually want to attend.
Everything after that is argument over whether the applicant brings something
fresh — a standard he openly calls flimsy. Lively essays are crowd-pleasers and
excerpts may be read aloud. He describes admission as effectively requiring
unanimity, which is harder than applicants assume given how differently officers
see things. Borderline cases consume the most discussion time; obvious admits
and obvious denials move fast.

**Connections.** Stated more bluntly than anywhere else in the corpus. His
example: a fight with a senior officer over a candidate whose father's friend
sat on the board — average statistics, a private campus tour, and admitted.

**Questbridge** he describes as close to a guarantee at top-7 schools given
supporting scores.

**International applicants.** His answer is bleak. Attend a feeder international
school, usually expensive and English-medium, or have family money. He says
perfect scores, grades, and activities otherwise count for very little, and that
he can't recall meeting a lower-middle-class international student at his school
who hadn't come through an elite private international school.

**Deferrals.** Most deferred applicants have little chance in the regular pool.
He tells one story anyway: a deferred applicant who wrote and illustrated a
children's book about the university, casting well-known alumni as characters,
and sent it in. He found it tacky. Senior staff loved it, she was admitted, and
the book was still in the office years later.

**Closing advice.** Elite colleges matter less than people think; only a handful
of industries care about undergraduate prestige; top graduate programs are
significantly easier to enter than top undergraduate ones. Divide what you're
evaluated on into things you control and things you don't, and ignore the second
category. He estimates that if someone tells you they went to Harvard, there's
maybe a coin flip's chance they earned it on merit. And no one who got in can
actually explain why — admissions sees applicants very differently from how
applicants see themselves.

**Miscellaneous.** ED gives a real advantage because the pool is weaker and
because roughly half the class may be filled that way; REA helps less because
those four pools contain the strongest applicants in the country. Twins tend to
do well and are often taken together. Transfers at the very top are largely
relationship-driven, roughly 10–20 a year.

## 2. "What Your Essay Looks Like From the Other Side" (Dec 2018)

Mostly captured in the lens. What remains useful:

**The base rate, stated plainly.** If a school admits 5% of applicants, then by
construction roughly 95% of essays are not good enough. Being better than 20% of
applicants and being better than 70% land in the same pile.

**Who should read your drafts.** He is unusually harsh here: former officers,
admitted students, and established consultants are worth listening to; friends,
high school teachers, and high school guidance counselors are not, and he claims
that a draft your teachers liked is statistically *more* likely to be weak than
strong. Treat this as self-interested — he sold essay review — but the
underlying point about audience mismatch is real.

**On complexity.** Don't build an elaborate extended metaphor. You're
seventeen and it will show. He also flags that sentence construction, tone, and
readability aren't taught in high school English, so most applicants lack the
tools.

**On structure.** He liked the idea of an adventure or quest shape because the
story tells itself, paired with deliberate vagueness early to pull the reader in.

## 3. "5 Most Overdone Essays and How to Avoid Them" (July 2019)

The five categories are in the lens. Two things aren't:

**Advice lag.** He opens by noting that essay advice circulating publicly runs
roughly five to six years behind reality — by 2019 nobody was writing about dead
pets or mission trips anymore, though the underlying instincts had simply
migrated to new topics.

**On the Costco essay.** Asked repeatedly about the viral essay that supposedly
got a student into five Ivies, his answer is a general principle: model yourself
on previous applicants only when their profile matches yours, since the essay
was doing work inside a file quite different from most readers'. His own verdict
on the essay was that it was fun and mildly refreshing, somewhat insincere and
over-written, and nowhere near the best he'd read.

# u/Aggravating_Humor

## 4. "What I've learned from reading tons of your essays" (Nov 2019)

Written as a Berkeley senior reviewing drafts posted to the subreddit —
*before* he worked in admissions. The five diagnoses are in the lens. Worth
keeping from the comment thread:

**The highlighter test** (from u/ScholarGrade, a verified consultant): go
through the draft and mark every sentence that says nothing about you. As you
cut for length, cut the marked sentences first. His reframing of the core
problem is sharper than the original post's — the issue isn't excessive detail,
it's detail that fails to advance plot, character, or insight.

**An MIT admissions officer's dissent.** u/peteyMIT disagreed with the claim
that hardship needs introspection to work: a challenge described plainly, with
no reflection attached, can be compelling and can supply useful context. His
objection is narrower — what grates is an applicant inflating something small,
or writing as though a single bad event defines their entire existence. He also
noted this is easier at MIT, where five short answers spread the load, and used
it to argue against the single-long-essay format generally.

**A parent's question about a hurricane.** A parent asked whether writing about
Hurricane Florence would seem legitimate given the family only evacuated two
days. The answer: it probably belongs in Additional Information rather than the
essay, and — usefully — officers are expected to know what happened in the
regions they read for. You don't need to establish that a regional disaster
occurred.

## 5. "Essays and how they're read at top schools" (2022)

The John Doe example and the in-context rule are in the lens. The comment
threads contain most of the value:

**Norming.** Officers are trained toward a shared sense of what strong looks
like. He reports rarely seeing a room genuinely split; more often the committee
converges, either on admitting or on the applicant not clearing the bar.

**Context is the operative concept, repeated relentlessly.** His paired example:
a student maxing out AP and dual enrollment at a rural high school while
contributing substantially to their community, versus a New York student with
National Youth Poet Laureate and strong grades but community college coursework.
Both are strong *in context*, and context is not stripped out at committee.

**A frustrated applicant's exchange** produced the clearest statement of what
"fit" means: not the school's culture, but whether the application coheres. His
elaboration is worth internalizing — a file made of unrelated strong pieces is
hard to pitch, and in a pool where forty students apply from the same school and
some of them make no mistakes, the ones who did get filtered out by competition
rather than by judgment. He also stresses that expectations scale to resources:
a well-funded suburban applicant gets less latitude on avoidable weaknesses than
someone from an underfunded school.

**Grades are supreme.** Pushed on whether the process is just vibes, he
conceded much of the critique but insisted grades set the direction of most
cases, and noted that no one is admitted on charm with a 2.0. Later data he
cites: Ivy admitted classes run roughly 60–75% students with 4.0 or above, and
95%+ in the top decile of their graduating class.

**Institutional priorities** are derived from analysis of previous admitted
classes — not a performance metric, but a real input to the following cycle.

**Upward trends** are good but don't erase earlier Bs. Whether they're
survivable depends entirely on what the rest of your school group looks like.

**Being the only applicant from your school.** The first questions are about
representation — have we admitted from this school before, how long has it been.
He's candid that this is largely lip service: a school that hasn't sent anyone
in years might get one admit and that's the end of it. He then evaluates in
context, reading the school profile, and — a detail that appears nowhere else —
looking the school up on Google Maps to see what the surrounding area is like,
which he says tells him a great deal.

**Grade granularity.** At his school there was no difference between an A- and
an A. There was a difference between a B- and a B. GPA usually taken from the
school as reported; the transcript matters more than the number.

**For a prospective historian**, asked how to distinguish oneself: Concord
Review publication, National History Day, writing that shows intellectual
seriousness, and an APUSH teacher recommendation that speaks to it. A commenter
immediately attacked the Concord Review as a pay-to-play operation with
publication fees and paid coaching, which is a fair caution.

## 7. AMA — Worked at an Ivy and another T20 office (June 2026)

The most substantive source in the corpus and the most recent.

**Territories.** Palo Alto and Mountain View, New York City, suburban and rural
Pennsylvania, parts of Los Angeles, plus China, India, Italy, Japan and others.
Two years at the Ivy, one at the other T20.

**The three things that dominate committee time**, asked directly, with essays
deliberately excluded because the question asked for three:

*Grades and rigor.* Unless the office knows a school intimately — and usually it
doesn't — the standard is whether you succeeded in the environment you were in.
He wants As, with plus and minus distinctions largely ignored, and the most
demanding schedule available. He specifically flags the counselor letter as the
place where rigor gets corroborated, since the counselor is the person best
positioned to say the schedule really was the hardest available.

*Superlative letters.* He prefers junior and senior year core-subject teachers,
because those classrooms are the closest available proxy for how you'd behave in
a college one. What he wants is explicit comparative language placing you among
the best of the year or the best in that teacher's career, backed by anecdotes.
What he doesn't want: a letter restating your activities, which the office
already has. Personal qualities are welcome but shouldn't crowd out classroom
observation.

*Activities showing commitment, leadership, and impact.* He says he doesn't care
what you do, only whether your time outside class is used meaningfully. The
famous programs stand out, but most admits don't have them. What he's looking
for is engagement with what was actually available to you, without blending into
what everyone around you does — and if you are doing the common thing, excelling
at it.

**Advice to a high school counselor** (a counselor asked directly, which makes
this the sharpest material on letters anywhere in the corpus): hand out
superlatives sparingly, because offices notice inflation and trust erodes across
cycles. The counselor's distinctive value is comparative — how does this student
stand relative to the graduating class academically, socially, emotionally,
intellectually. He explicitly doesn't want a counselor letter that dwells on one
activity or on personal qualities alone; he wants the high-level view, since
teacher letters cover the classroom and the student's own essays cover
interiority. On negative information: he says offices genuinely want candor,
frames the counselor as the office's proxy on the ground, and asks for
proportionality — a small blip probably isn't worth including.

**Things he says are false that applicants believe:**

*Passion projects.* He calls the term consultant vocabulary that offices don't
use, and says no one is admitted for having founded something. He'd rather a
student join people already working on a problem than reinvent an organization,
or simply spend the time on something else.

*Research as a requirement.* Not necessary, and he says he doesn't understand
where the belief came from. He flags a second trap: applicants writing about
research in dense technical language, when readers are generalists and won't
follow it — Caltech being an exception where faculty read closely.

*That only extraordinary applicants get in.* Context governs. He describes
students who believe they're exceptional without understanding their regional
pool, and students who write themselves off entirely because they aren't at
olympiad level.

**Supplemental portfolios.** Submitting one as a non-major can't hurt; a weak
faculty rating is equivalent to not submitting, since it was optional. Very low
ratings are rare. He's never personally seen a strong faculty review rescue a
weaker file.

**Committee.** The chair holds the pool-level view by design, because individual
officers only know their own regions. One denial reason he names that applicants
don't anticipate: the class already contains this profile, which the presenting
officer has no way of knowing.

**Institutional priorities.** Academics is always first. Broad ones (more
engineering students) hold steady year to year. Granular ones change annually —
he mentions a spreadsheet specifying which instruments the orchestra wants.

**Intellectual risk-taking**, asked what it means for STEM applicants: moving
into territory you don't understand and trying ideas that might fail, working in
undefined spaces, asking questions you'd previously avoided.

**Humor.** Be funny if you're funny; write as yourself because it's easier than
sustaining a persona. He recounts presenting an applicant whose babysitting
essay genuinely made him laugh and whose character came through completely — and
the committee denied them anyway. Nothing in an essay secures admission.

**Social media and GitHub** are rarely checked. Some officers are curious; most
aren't.

**What sticks after forty files in a day:** letters and essays, usually
together. Specific phrases rarely. A distinctive way of seeing the world,
reliably.

**Reading order.** He filtered by high school and read alphabetically within
each. Most officers work school group by school group, ordering however they
prefer.

**Below-median scores.** No automatic denial. What happens is quieter: he
mentally flags the applicant as unlikely to be competitive and then reads the
rest of the file quickly, mostly skimming, looking for context in Additional
Information or the letters. Sub-1500 needs strong grades, maximum rigor, and
something genuinely distinguishing.

**Private school counseling politics.** He confirms that offices see shuffling —
counselors steering students toward and away from particular schools to spread
outcomes across the class. His own consulting client at an elite Bay Area school
was strongly discouraged from applying to Harvard, applied anyway, and was
admitted in the restrictive early round. He also describes counselors at some
schools refusing to help students, and notes those counselors change behavior
quickly when a student cites professional obligations.

**Reapplying after a gap year.** The old file is sometimes attached but rarely
reopened; he reports no bias from the previous denial and thinks the odds of an
officer remembering you are slim. Gap year applications are generally more
polished — cleaner essays, clearer academic purpose.

**On financial aid** (from a commenter he endorsed): students often stay on
waitlists because their existing offers aren't actually affordable, and
counselors who restrict application counts can materially damage a family's
ability to find an affordable option. Worth knowing if a counselor pushes back
on list length.

**Ivy-specific fit.** He resists the idea that each Ivy wants a personality
type — no officer says a student needs to be quirkier or to like hiking more.
What he offers instead are curricular questions: Columbia expects you to
actually want to read, given the core; Brown expects you to understand what an
open curriculum is for; Dartmouth expects familiarity with the liberal arts and
the D-plan; Yale wants students who pursue several unrelated things at once. He
stresses these aren't requirements.

**Bay Area advice.** The pool is STEM-saturated. One way to stand out is not to
be that, though he acknowledges this isn't always practical or desired. The real
question is who specifically you're competing against at your own school.

**Transfers.** College grades dominate, since they're direct evidence of
performance at college level; high school grades fade as you go from sophomore
to junior transfer. He wants a concrete academic reason to transfer and warns
against criticizing your current school, which reads as complaining. Campus or
off-campus involvement both count. HYPSM transfer rates around 1%.

**Class rank at feeder schools.** Being academically strongest matters, and he
recognizes rigor at schools like Thomas Jefferson. No one is an automatic admit.
Where a counselor can add value is clarifying who genuinely stands out.

**Test scores generally.** Above the 50th percentile they blur together. He
notes older officers still visibly react to a 1590 on a first attempt, which he
treats as mild rather than decisive.
