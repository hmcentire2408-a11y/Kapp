# The Reader's Lens: An Evaluation Protocol for College Essays

*Synthesized from: former AO accounts (u/williamthereader, u/Aggravating_Humor), Harvard's Class of 2023 Reading Procedures (SFFA trial exhibit P633), and Sukumar et al., "Making a Pecan Pie: Understanding and Supporting the Holistic Review Process in Admissions" (CSCW 2018).*

---

## 0. The frame: what an essay is actually for

Most essay advice treats the essay as a piece of writing to be judged. It isn't. It is an **input to a rating** and **raw material for a verbal pitch**. Three distinct jobs, in descending order of how much they actually decide outcomes:

**Job 1 — Feed the Personal rating.** Harvard's reading procedures define the Personal rating as "an assessment made by the readers of what kind of effect the student might have on others at Harvard and beyond... what kind of contribution would the person make to the dining hall conversation, to study groups, and to society as a whole after graduation." The essay is the student's only direct contribution to this rating. Crucially, the rubric explicitly says "characteristics not always synonymous with extroversion are similarly valued. Applicants who seem to be particularly reflective, insightful and/or dedicated should receive higher personal ratings."

That last sentence is the closest thing to an official rubric that exists. **Reflective, insightful, dedicated.** Not clever, not funny, not lyrical.

**Job 2 — Survive compression into a decision sheet.** The CSCW study found that reviewers' actual reasoning "amounted to more than the brief summaries they entered in the decision sheet" — and that this reasoning "would have long since been forgotten during committee meetings," at which point "the reviewers mostly rely on the decision sheet to recall an application." The study also records the vocabulary readers use to summarize essays: they "build a character profile of the applicant based on the essays, e.g., 'vulnerable,' 'risk-taker,' 'perfectionist,' 'uptight.'"

So: your essay will be reduced to two or three adjectives written by a stranger, and *those adjectives*, not your prose, are what enters committee.

**Job 3 — Become a pitchable script.** From the AO account: the regional officer stands up and describes the applicant. "A bad pitch can ruin a good application, but a good pitch can really make the difference for a so-so application." In the case where it worked, the officer "quote[d] one of the more profound lines in her conclusion." The CSCW paper independently confirms the mechanism and names its bias: **narrative fallacy** — "a tendency to present 'tidy,' coherent stories of applicants."

The machine rewards tidy coherent stories. This is not a reason to fabricate one; it is a reason to make sure the true one is legible.

## 1. The context rule (read this before evaluating anything)

**An essay has no score in isolation.** This is the single most important correction to how essays get discussed online.

From the T10/Ivy AO: "officers don't assign ratings to essays. Not the top schools I'm familiar with at least." An essay "can be a 10/10 or a 4/10, depending on various factors in the application." Their worked example — "John Doe," great bookstore essay, undermined by a lukewarm English teacher letter and an awkward interview — ends in a denial not because the essay was bad but because "all of his app together just didn't fit together."

The CSCW paper explains the mechanism: **anchoring** ("attribute encountered (usually academic) prior to the current one may influence the evaluation of the current one") and **confirmation bias** ("reviewers find and favor evidence that confirms their judgments"). Readers hit academics first. By the time they reach the essay, they have a hypothesis. The essay is read *for confirmation*.

Two consequences for evaluation:

- **Never rate an essay without the app around it.** Ask what impression the transcript and school context will have already formed, and whether this essay confirms or contradicts it. Contradiction reads as inauthenticity, not range.
- **The comparison set is the school group.** Committee screens list "applicants ordered by school and by GPA within each school." The relevant question is never "is this good?" It is "is this more compelling than the other students from this high school applying to this college?"

## 2. The screens, in order

Run a draft through these in sequence. Failing an early screen makes later ones moot.

### Screen 1 — The corroboration test
Does anything in this essay claim a quality that the rest of the file will not independently confirm?

Harvard rates "School Support" on a scale where 1 is "Strikingly unusual support. 'The best of a career,' 'one of the best in many years'" and 3 is "Generally positive, perhaps somewhat neutral or generic." An essay that presents you as a formidable intellect, paired with a 3, is worse than no essay — it creates the "weird taste in my mouth" the AO described.

**Ask:** which teacher, which activity, which grade backs each claim? If the answer is "nothing," cut the claim or change the claim.

### Screen 2 — The payload test
Highlight every sentence that reveals nothing about the writer. Cut those first.

The canonical failure: *"My heart raced as the door shut. It was 2 AM..."* — the reviewer's verdict was that "the writing itself is fine, the problem is the fact that I learn nothing about you." Detail is not the enemy; **detail that doesn't advance character is.**

**Threshold:** if more than ~35% of the word count is scene-setting that survives deletion without loss, the essay is narrative-heavy in the way that reads as a 2019 template.

### Screen 3 — The structural fingerprint test
The most-flagged structure in the corpus, verbatim:

1. Standalone attention-grabbing sentence
2. Paragraph establishing context and situation
3. Montage of the journey to this moment
4. Return to present, resolution
5. Personal takeaway indistinguishable from a hundred others

If a draft maps cleanly onto all five beats in order, it is invisible. The fix is not to abandon narrative but to break the sequence — withhold, reorder, start mid-argument, or let the takeaway be unresolved.

### Screen 4 — The adjective test
Write down the two or three words a tired reader would put in the decision sheet after this essay. Then check them against the Personal rating language: does the list include *reflective*, *insightful*, *dedicated*, *compassionate*, *resilient*? Or does it include *uptight*, *perfectionist*, *contrived*, *pleasant*?

If you can't generate the adjectives, neither can the reader, and the essay produces nothing.

### Screen 5 — The pitch test
Can a stranger who has read this once, alongside forty others, stand up and describe the applicant for sixty seconds with enthusiasm — without rereading?

Sub-tests:
- **Is there a quotable line?** The successful committee pitch involved quoting the conclusion. Most essays contain no sentence worth repeating aloud.
- **Is it retrievable?** CSCW names availability bias: what is easily recalled dominates. Concrete, unusual, specific things are retrievable. Abstractions are not.
- **Does it survive summary?** If the essay's effect depends on its prose rhythm, it dies at the decision-sheet stage.

### Screen 6 — The sincerity test
"As soon as I get the feeling that someone is being insincere, I don't read the rest of their essay."

The named rejection triggers: imposed moral superiority, thesaurus reach, grandiose extended metaphor ("Don't write me some extended grandiose metaphor. You're 17"), and self-aggrandizement. The AO who was asked what unites flawless-on-paper applicants rejected everywhere answered: "arrogance and inconsistency in writing (style and content)."

The date test still works: read it aloud and ask whether you'd want a second date with the person who said this.

## 3. The overdone catalog

Ranked by frequency in the source AO's experience, with the actual objection rather than the topic label:

| Pattern | The real objection | Salvage condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Standard 5-beat narrative** | Not the topic — the *identical* architecture, plus "nothing of substance to say" | Break the beat sequence; earn the takeaway |
| **First-world woe-is-me** | Assumes sympathy converts to admission; readers can infer income from activities and region. "At worst, they think you're exaggerating your circumstances" | Only if the difficulty produces a genuinely non-obvious idea |
| **Quirky without substance** | Object-as-personality. "I can't differentiate you from other students if 75% of you like oreos" | The quirk must change how you *think*, demonstrably |
| **Tragedy** | Three failures: commonness, "you spend the entire essay talking about other people rather than yourself," and no new observation | "should not be the focal point... weave it into a larger structure" |
| **Food / recipe** | The layers-of-a-lasagna metaphor is now a genre | Rarely |
| **Sports / music / MUN / debate / research** | Generic unless you're elite at it or have an unusual angle | "if you're a beast at them or have a unique perspective" |
| **Jargon-heavy research** | "AOs don't necessarily specialize in specific subjects... chances are that we won't totally follow" | Translate or don't |

**On tragedy specifically**, because it's the highest-stakes case: the objection is structural, not moral. An essay that is 90% event and 10% reflection fails; the reader learns about the person who died, not the person applying. Meanwhile the *fact* of the loss belongs elsewhere in the file — Harvard codes "substantial commitment outside of conventional extracurricular activities such as family obligations" directly into the extracurricular rating, and the counselor letter and Additional Information section exist precisely to carry context. Putting the fact where it counts frees the essay to do a different job.

## 4. Positive markers (rarer, and worth naming)

Things the sources report actually working:

- **Mundane subject, unusual mind.** The rural-Michigan movie-theater essay: "Nothing special about the essay but it was something they were passionate about and it was done well... It felt sincere."
- **Withheld information / non-obvious opening.** "adding a little mystery / intrigue. Being purposefully vague in the beginning and drawing the reader in... Don't make it predictable."
- **Humor, used sparingly.** "the most underutilized tool," but "2-3 jokes max... Start your essay with a light tone and end with something thoughtful/poignant." Also: humor alone secures nothing — the AO recalls laughing at a babysitting essay and still denying the applicant.
- **A distinctive way of seeing.** Asked what sticks after forty files: "Specific ways someone viewed the world, especially if it's super different from everyone else, always stuck with me."
- **Simplicity.** "The best essays I've ever read were simple in design."

## 5. Evaluation protocol (use this on a draft)

1. **Restate the file first.** Before reading the essay, state what the transcript, school profile, activities, and likely LORs will have already established. That's the anchor.
2. **Read once at speed.** Then, without rereading, write: the two-to-three adjectives, the one-sentence pitch, and the quotable line (or note its absence). This simulates the decision sheet.
3. **Run screens 1–6.** Report failures with the specific sentence or passage responsible.
4. **Locate it in the overdone catalog.** Name the pattern if it matches, and state the salvage condition.
5. **Give the in-context verdict.** Not "this is a 7/10" — instead: "against the other NCSSM-Morganton applicants to this school, does this essay make the officer's pitch easier or harder, and why?"
6. **Calibrate honestly.** The stated base rates: "85% of all essays are 1) mediocrely written and 2) boring"; roughly 1 in 15 stand out to a given reader. A draft that reads as competent and pleasant is, by the numbers, in the failing majority. Say so.

## 6. Standing cautions for whoever is using this lens

- **Don't over-fit to any one reader.** Every source hedges: "these reflect my own experience, and I'll bet that another reader in another office might have a different set of essays he/she/they are tired of reading." Two of these accounts explicitly disagree about whether a plainly-described hardship needs introspection to work (an MIT AO pushed back: "a challenge, clearly described without introspection (necessarily), can be very compelling and provide useful context").
- **These are anonymous accounts.** Two are pseudonymous former AOs who are now paid consultants — a commercial interest in the process seeming decipherable. The Harvard procedures and the CSCW paper are the load-bearing documents; the Reddit material is texture that happens to corroborate them on the mechanics that matter (compression, committee pitch, in-context comparison).
- **Academics outranks all of this.** The Ivy AO, asked for the top three things: grades and rigor, superlative LORs, activities showing commitment — and then, "I didn't add essays because you asked for three." Harvard's own committee sequence puts academic performance third, ahead of essays at twelfth. An essay cannot rescue a file that loses on academics within its school group; it can only decide between files that don't.
